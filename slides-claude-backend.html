Haiku 4.5 vs Opus 5: what the numbers actually say

Seven dispatch strategies over the same 36-task workload, every call charged to the strategy that made it. Cost and latency are measured precisely. Accuracy is not — and the gap between those two facts is the whole result.

Run live, --mode record Calls 555 Spend $0.2572 Tasks 36 (12 easy / 12 medium / 12 hard) Models claude-haiku-4-5 · claude-opus-5

What the seven strategies are

Two of these rows are baselines. The rest are attempts to land inside the gap between them — and there are only two ways to try: guess before you generate, or check after.

The column that tells the story is Sent to Opus. All three guess-before routers land at 58–67%: when unsure, they panic and pay up, which is why none saves more than a third. The cascade sends 14% — not because it guesses better, but because it does not guess at all.

The trap is the obvious design. “Ask a smart model which model to use” comes last on every axis: dearest of the routers, slowest at 3,685 ms, and 18.1% of its budget spent deciding rather than answering.

Overhead is the share of spend that bought no part of the returned answer: the classifier's own tokens, the cascade's verification call, and cheap attempts discarded on escalation.

Read the CI column, not the accuracy column. Every interval overlaps every other. The cascade tops the accuracy column by two tasks out of thirty-six — the next section takes those two apart, and one of them is a coin flip.

Why does the cascade top the accuracy column?

It shouldn't, and the honest answer is that it doesn't really. A strategy that sends 86% of traffic to the cheap model has no business out-scoring the expensive one. The entire margin is two tasks out of thirty-six, and it dissolves when you look at them.

h12wronghaiku rightwrong Verifier miss — accepted a wrong Haiku answer, so it never escalated.

Three wins minus one loss is +2 tasks = +5.6 points, and that is the whole of “97.2% vs 91.7%”. Of the three wins: one clean, one a safety-classifier quirk, one a coin flip. This is precisely why the paired test returns p = 0.625.

Do not present the accuracy column as a ranking. At 36 tasks it is noise sorted by luck. The columns that carry real signal are cost (exact, from billed tokens), latency (wall-clock), and % to Opus (a count, not an estimate). If someone asks “so the cascade is more accurate?”, the correct answer is “we can't tell, and neither can anyone else at this sample size.”

The one thing this does establish: the cascade did not lose accuracy while cutting cost 59%. “No detected regression” is a real and useful claim. “Better” is not.

Performance

Latency is the axis dispatch usually makes worse. The cascade pays for an extra round trip on every request; the LLM classifier pays for two.

The cascade lands at 2,735 ms — essentially always-Opus latency (2,769 ms) for 41% of always-Opus cost. It buys the saving with round trips, not with a faster model. The classifier is the worst of both: slowest and second-most expensive.

If time-to-first-token matters, cascade is the wrong tool. It cannot stream — the verifier needs the complete cheap answer before it can judge, so a streamed cascade would buffer the whole response. Streaming traffic falls back to the zero-token heuristic, which decides before any tokens are generated.

Note the row that ruins a tidy story: Opus scores 92% on easy, below Haiku's 100%. One of those misses is a safety refusal — Opus 5 returned stop_reason: "refusal", category cyber, on "what exit code does a POSIX shell return when a command is not found?" Haiku answered it fine. Tier dispatch changes your refusal surface, not just your bill.

The cost column has no error bars. It is arithmetic over billed tokens from response.usage. The accuracy column has a ±9–14 point interval. So the only defensible claim is “59% cheaper with no detected quality regression” — never “cheaper and better”.

An escalation costs 18× a clean cascade hit and takes ~1.7× as long. That ratio is why the escalation rate — 14% here, against a true need of 14% — is the number to watch in production, not the average.

And the lever that beat all of this. Driving the same Claude backend from Codex CLI, whose harness resends a 13,443-token prompt every turn: caching that prefix took the steady-state turn from $0.069 to $0.0155 — 78% off, no model change, no quality risk. The router sent every one of those requests to Opus anyway, because a 13k prompt trips every length signal there is.

Ranked by what the numbers support: fix caching first (78%, no quality risk, measured). Then cascade dispatch (59%, no detected regression, +100% latency). Do not build the LLM classifier (17.5%, slowest, 18% of its budget spent deciding).

Every figure here comes from a live run — 555 calls, $0.2572 of real spend, on claude-haiku-4-5 and claude-opus-5. Cost is computed from response.usage with cache reads at 0.10× and writes at 1.25×; latency is wall-clock across all legs; accuracy is deterministic short-answer grading with the confidence intervals shown.

Limits. 36 tasks, one workload, one run, short-answer grading only. Model nondeterminism means the “oracle” row is not a strict upper bound — it probes models in sequence and can lose a task another strategy happened to win. Reproduce with uv run dms bench --two-tier --mode record.
