Engineering share · measured on 36 tasks, $0.50 of API spend

Is a high/low model dispatcher worth building?

One decision per request: send it to the cheap model or the expensive one. We built five dispatchers, ran them against Haiku 4.5 and Opus 5 on the same workload, and charged every dispatcher for its own tokens. The cost savings are real and large. Not one of the quality differences was statistically detectable — including whether the expensive model is better at all.

01 / THE CEILING

Only 19% of the workload is routable at all

Before evaluating any dispatcher, measure how often the two models actually disagree. Where they agree, the dispatcher cannot change quality — only cost. That share is a hard ceiling on dispatch value, and it is a property of your workload, not of your router.

81% agreement. A perfect dispatcher and a coin produce the same answer on four requests in five. Everything router research argues about lives in the remaining 19% — and a fifth of that runs backwards, where the cheap model is right and the expensive one is not.

Do this first. Run both models over ~200 real requests and count disagreements. It costs a few dollars, takes an afternoon, and tells you the ceiling before you write a line of routing logic.

04 / WHICH DISPATCHER

Decide after the cheap answer, not before it

The pre-request routers all converge on the same failure: when unsure, send it to the expensive model. Two of them pushed 58% of traffic to Opus and kept only a third of the saving. The cascade routes on evidence instead of prediction.

Cascade — post-hoc

−59%

Answer cheap, verify, escalate on doubt. Sent only 14% to Opus. Pays for the discarded answer and a verification call on every request — 31.9% of its budget bought no answer — and still won on cost by a wide margin.

Heuristic — ex-ante, free

−34%

Seven regex signals, zero tokens, sub-millisecond. Best of the pre-request routers, and the one you can read in a code review.

LLM classifier — ex-ante, paid

−18%

Spends 18.1% of its own budget deciding, then sends 67% to Opus anyway. The worst deal on the board: most of the cost of always-Opus, none of the simplicity.

Measured

The lexical router scored worse than a coin flipping at its own rate (−1.2% accuracy for +$0.005). It routed on surface word overlap, which correlates with topic, not difficulty. A dispatcher that is not better than random is not a dispatcher.

Why post-hoc wins. Routing needs a good prediction of quality before spending anything; cascading needs a judgement of quality after one cheap answer exists. The second problem is much easier — and cheap answers are cheap. Removing the verifier collapses the cascade straight back into always-Haiku, so the verifier is the entire mechanism.

05 / WHAT BIT US

Three failure modes we hit in 555 calls

The high model refused an easy question

Opus 5 returned stop_reason: "refusal", category cyber, on: “In POSIX shells, what exit code is returned when a command is not found?” Haiku answered it fine. Empty content, zero billed output, and it grades as a wrong answer unless you check. Tier dispatch changes your refusal surface, not just your bill.

A token cap masquerading as a wrong answer

Opus 5 thinks by default and max_tokens caps thinking plus text. Our first run capped answers at 512 and truncated the expensive model into looking incompetent. Raising the cap fixed it. Always separate stop_reason from correctness.

A router keyed on a spurious feature

Our first heuristic scored on “answer with the number only” — a phrase present at every difficulty. It routed 100% of traffic to Haiku and read perfectly sensibly in review. Surface form is not difficulty.

All three grade as “the model got it wrong” unless you instrument for them specifically. Two of the three made the expensive model look worse than it is, which is the direction that quietly justifies a bad routing decision.

06 / THE BIGGER LEVER

Caching pays more than dispatch, and costs no quality

Dispatch trades quality risk for money. Prompt caching does not — same model, same answers, smaller bill. On repeated-prefix workloads it also beats dispatch outright.

Exact arithmetic

Two facts that make dispatch and caching fight each other. A model switch invalidates the prompt cache completely and has no escape hatch, because caches are model-scoped. And the cache floor is not monotonic with price — Opus 5 caches from 512 tokens, Haiku 4.5 needs 4,096, so a 3k prompt caches on the expensive model and silently does not on the cheap one. Past step 37 of a loop, cached Opus is cheaper than uncached Haiku outright.

Takeaway. Fix caching before you argue about model choice. It is the only lever on the list with no quality risk to measure, and on long loops it beats dispatch anyway.

The dispatcher, running

Every response carries a dms_dispatch block naming the model that actually answered, the reason, per-leg spend, and the overhead. Without it you cannot detect the silent regression.

Streaming can't cascade

The verifier needs the complete cheap answer, so a streamed cascade would buffer everything and destroy time-to-first-token. Streaming falls back to the zero-token heuristic. A real trade, not a gap.

Session affinity is on

A model switch invalidates the prompt cache with no escape hatch. A reroute forfeits the 0.10× read and re-pays the 1.25× write, so the first decision sticks for an hour.

Then we pointed Codex at it

Codex resends a 13,443-token harness prompt every turn. Uncached: $0.069 a turn, and the router sent it to Opus regardless because a 13k prompt trips every length signal. Caching the prefix took the steady-state turn to $0.0155 — 78% off, no model change. The first turn cost more ($0.0817, the 1.25× write premium); break-even is turn two.

A refusal retries the other tier

When the high model refuses, the cheap answer already paid for is returned rather than discarded. Measured: Opus refused a benign shell question Haiku answered fine.

Observed in production shape

It also failed live, exactly as measured. Asked an LRU-cache question, Haiku answered 4, the verifier accepted, and the correct answer is 5. That is the 67%-recall verifier miss, in the wild. The cascade is 59% cheaper — it is not more accurate.

Takeaway. The router was an afternoon. The measurement that told us which router, and the eval that would catch it regressing, are the actual project.

08 / THE ANSWER

Worth building — for the cost, and only with an eval

  1. Measure your disagreement rate first. Both models over ~200 real requests. If they agree 95% of the time, a dispatcher is a cost optimisation and nothing more — scope it that way. Ours was 81%.
  2. Fix caching before dispatch. Bigger saving, no quality risk, and a reroute forfeits the cache you just built.
  3. If you build one, build a cascade. Cheap answer, verify, escalate. 59% cheaper than always-high here, decides on evidence rather than prediction, and degrades to always-cheap rather than always-expensive when the verifier is weak.
  4. Do not build an LLM classifier. It was the most expensive dispatcher and the least effective — 18% of its budget spent deciding, and it still sent two-thirds of traffic to the expensive model.
  5. Budget for the eval, not the router. Several hundred labelled tasks to detect a 5-point regression. The router is an afternoon; the eval is the project.
  6. Claim cost, not quality. “59% cheaper with no detected regression” is defensible and useful. “Cheaper and better” is not, on any sample size a team this size will actually build.

The one-line version. A high/low dispatcher is worth roughly a third to a half off your bill, capped by a disagreement rate you can measure in an afternoon — and the quality question is far harder to answer than the routing question.

How to read the badges. Measured means it came from response.usage on a live run — 555 calls, $0.2572 of real spend — or is arithmetic over published per-token rates. Cost figures carry no sampling error; accuracy figures carry the confidence intervals shown. Literature marks someone else's published number.

Known limits. 36 tasks, short-answer grading only, one workload, one run. Model nondeterminism means the “oracle” row is not a strict upper bound — it probes models sequentially and can lose a task another strategy happened to win. Everything is reproducible: uv run dms bench --two-tier, uv run dms levers, uv run pytest (133 tests).
